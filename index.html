Why now.

Language tools have changed faster than AAC ever has. Cost, learning curve, and limited language depth are the rest of the gap. We think there is a window to do something about it, with the people who live with AAC every day in the room while it gets built.

What could be possible.

If language tools moved as fast as language itself, here is what some of the people who live with AAC might notice.

For an AAC user

Imagine if the next word was usually the one you were already reaching for, so a sentence felt like a sentence and not a search.

For a family member

Imagine if you could be present with your kid in a conversation, instead of disappearing into menus while the moment passes.

For a school SLP

Imagine if you spent more of your week with the students on your caseload, and less of it reconstructing what happened in the room from someone else's notes.

For a district administrator

Imagine if "is every kid we are responsible for actually being heard" was a question you could answer with evidence, not a hope you carry into board meetings.

Privacy is the line.

Ethical AI in AAC means no training on user communication, no sale of vocabulary patterns, and no quiet data harvesting. The hard question is how to use modern language tools to remove the speed, cost, and learning-curve barriers in AAC without crossing that line.

We do not have every answer yet. We have principles, and we are looking for AAC users, SLPs, families, and administrators to help us find the rest.

Help us figure this out.

These are real questions we are working through. We have principles. We do not have every answer, and we would rather build with people in the room than guess.

How do we see a student's progress without watching every word they say?

A kid who stalls in October should be found in November, not in May at the IEP. That argues for visibility.

The same kid deserves a system that is not also surveillance. That argues for restraint.

Most of the field has picked one and lived with the trade. We think there is a more honest answer in the middle.

Help us find it.

Where is the line for AI-generated language in a classroom?

A teacher needs a vocabulary set for Monday's solar-system lesson by Sunday night. Real case.

A student reaches for a word the system suggested but did not mean. Also real.

Both can be true on the same screen. We are looking for the review process, the consent model, and the visual cues that let a generated board be useful and still keep the student's voice the student's voice.

Help us design it.
